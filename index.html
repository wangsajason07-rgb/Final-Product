03 — Problem Statement

The research question and what the evidence shows

Research Question
"Can Physical Internet hubs be implemented in Dutch ports within a 12-month deployment timeline given current autonomous vehicle regulations?"
Hypothesis Confirmed
Dutch regulatory barriers — the RDW case-by-case approval process (R1), the unresolved Vienna Convention Article 8 gap (R2), and the absent liability framework (R3/R4) — are the primary obstacle. More significant than technical or economic constraints.
Core Gap
NL scores 0/10 on the Readiness Index — the only country where no regulatory dimension reaches even a partial score. Germany (8/10) and Sweden (6/10) prove deployment is achievable. The NL gap is a policy choice, not a technical constraint.
Evidence Base
Document analysis (20 sources, R1–R4/T/E/C/S coding) · Comparative case analysis (NL vs Germany vs Sweden) · Regulatory Readiness Index
Urgency
The European Commission targets hub-to-hub L4 freight approval at EU level for 2026. Germany's A9 corridor L4 truck trials are already active. Without action, NL risks losing its position as Europe's leading logistics hub.
04 — Key Research Findings

Five findings. One clear answer.

Document analysis, comparative case, and readiness index all converge: the barrier is regulatory, not technical.

F1
Primary Barrier · R1 · 55% of sources

The RDW case-by-case process is the operational bottleneck

Coded R1 in 11 of 20 sources. Tesla FSD: 18+ months pending March 2026. Single-operator exemptions take 6–12 months (TNO, 2025). PI hub multi-operator context would multiply this timeline under current rules.

RDW (2025) · TNO (2025) · Electrek (2026) · Galassi et al. (2023)
F2
Vienna Convention · R2 · 35% of sources

Article 8 gap unresolved in Dutch national law

Vellinga (2019): an AV has no 'driver' under Art. 1(v). Germany resolved this via Technical Supervisor role in 2021 Act. RDW explicitly prohibits remote operation without a safety driver on site.

Vellinga (2019) · WEF (2019) · German AV Act (2021) · RDW (2025)
F3
Circular Barrier · R3/R4

Liability-insurance loop reinforces the blockage

No L4 liability rule → insurers cannot price policies → operators cannot deploy → no actuarial data → loop continues. Resolving R1 breaks the loop by generating deployment data.

Tjong Tjin Tai (2024) · Munich Re (n.d.) · Tjong Tjin Tai (2018)
F4
Technology Ready · T · 6 sources

Technology is NOT the constraint

Einride L4 trucks commercially deployed on fixed EU routes, RISE-audited (2024). Milakis et al. (2017): NL deployment divergence driven entirely by regulatory approach, not technical capability.

Einride/RISE (2024) · Milakis et al. (2017) · TNO (2025) · WEF (2025)
F5
Counter-Evidence — Refines Hypothesis

Stakeholders support regulation — if coordinated

Van Duin et al. (2023): stakeholders positive toward regulation if nationally coordinated. The problem is fragmentation, not regulation. Germany and Sweden both regulate — predictably. NL regulates case-by-case.

05 — Project Purpose

Two phases. One goal.

This project proposes a phased regulatory reform pathway to enable Physical Internet hub deployment in Dutch ports. It translates Deep Dive research into a structured, actionable proposal for the Ministry of IenW, Port of Rotterdam Authority, and RDW.

Phase 1 establishes a regulatory sandbox at Rotterdam's Maasvlakte within 12 months — enabling the first multi-operator autonomous vehicle hub-to-hub operations in the Netherlands under controlled conditions. Phase 2 converts sandbox learnings into permanent national legislation within 24 months, bringing the Netherlands' Regulatory Readiness Index from 0/10 to 9/10.

The project does not propose new technology development. The technology exists and is commercially deployed in Sweden (Einride/RISE, 2024) and operationally tested in Germany (TNO/MAGPIE). The project proposes the specific regulatory and legislative changes needed to allow that technology to operate commercially in Dutch port zones.

08 — Stakeholder Overview

Who needs to act — and why they should care

Each stakeholder has a different reason to support this. The argument that works for IenW is not the same argument that works for RDW or Port of Rotterdam. This section addresses each one directly.

🏛️
Ministry of IenW
Primary legislative authority
Germany passed its L4 AV law in 2021. The European Commission has set 2026 as the hub-to-hub deployment target. Every month the Netherlands delays is another month Rotterdam loses ground to Hamburg and Bremen as the preferred EU port for autonomous freight operations.
Draft L4 AV law (Action 2.1). Approve Maasvlakte operational area designation (Action 1.1).
📋
RDW
Approval authority · Fast-track owner
The case-by-case approval process is cited in 55% of sources as the primary deployment barrier. A standardised fast-track template for port zones does not reduce safety standards — it reduces administrative unpredictability, which is what operators and investors need to commit capital.
Issue standardised fast-track template (Action 1.2). Co-design multi-operator approval pathway.
⚓
Port of Rotterdam
Pilot host · Infrastructure provider
APM Maasvlakte already runs autonomous terminal tractors. MAGPIE and CAT4Yards are active. The infrastructure and trials are in place. What is missing is the regulatory designation that turns a trial into a commercial operation — and that designation is what keeps Rotterdam competitive with Hamburg and Bremerhaven long-term.
Designate APM Maasvlakte as sandbox zone. Integrate MAGPIE/CAT4Yards into Phase 1.3 pilot.
🔬
TNO
Research & technical validation
TNO has already confirmed L4 readiness in controlled port environments. The sandbox generates the actuarial data that breaks the circular insurance barrier. Without that data, no insurer can price L4 coverage — and without coverage, no operator can deploy at commercial scale.
Safety validation for sandbox. Generate actuarial data to activate insurance market. Advise RDW on fast-track design.
🚛
Logistics operators & AV manufacturers
End users · Technology providers
Van Duin et al. (2023) confirmed that operators support AV integration — provided the regulatory framework is nationally coordinated and not imposed piecemeal. The Phase 1 sandbox is exactly that: a nationally coordinated, predictable pathway that lets operators plan deployments without 18-month approval uncertainty.
Participate in Phase 1.3 pilot. Provide deployment data. Engage with RDW on fast-track template design.
🛡️
Insurance market
Risk coverage enabler
The circular barrier is real: no insurer will offer L4 coverage without actuarial data, and no data exists without deployment. The Phase 1 sandbox breaks this loop by generating the first Dutch L4 port operations dataset. Whichever insurer engages early positions itself as the market leader in a product category that will scale across Europe by 2027.
Engage in Phase 1 pilot to develop initial L4 coverage products once sandbox generates actuarial data.

14 — International Precedent

Exactly what Germany and Sweden did — step by step

Both countries solved the same problem NL faces today. The mechanisms are documented and repeatable. This is the direct model for the proposed NL reform.

🇩🇪
Germany
Score: 8/10 — Deployment Ready
2017
Federal government commissions BMVI study on AV legal framework gaps. Vienna Convention issue identified as primary legislative obstacle.
2019
Ethics Commission on Automated and Connected Driving publishes framework. Establishes principle that Technical Supervisor (not driver) is legally responsible for L4 systems.
2021
Autonomous Driving Act (Gesetz) enacted — world first. Creates Technical Supervisor role, permits L4 in defined operational areas, resolves Vienna Convention Art. 8 without international amendment.
2022
AFGBV operational ordinance published. Defines approval process, safety requirements, and Technical Supervisor responsibilities. State authorities designated as approval bodies.
2023+
MAN and TRATON running active L4 truck trials on A9 corridor. Hamburg and Bremen port terminals approved as L4 operational areas. Insurance market products available.
NL equivalent: Actions 2.1 + 2.2 replicate this exactly — dedicated L4 law + WVW amendment with Technical Operator role. Timeline: 18–24 months.
🇸🇪
Sweden
Score: 6/10 — Conditionally Ready
2016
Swedish Transport Agency (Transportstyrelsen) begins consultation on AV trial permit framework. Applicant-led safety case model proposed.
2019
Einride begins remote-controlled electric freight truck operations on public road — first in EU. Initial permit granted under existing road traffic exemption framework.
2021
TSFS 2021:4 regulations published — trial permit framework for automated vehicles. Applicant submits self-assessment safety case. 3–6 month review. No dedicated L4 law required.
2022
Einride scales to commercial L4 freight operations on fixed EU routes. RISE (Research Institutes of Sweden) conducts independent safety audit — published 2024.
2024
RISE audit confirms safety case methodology. Insurance market actively engaged. Sweden becomes first country with independently-audited commercial L4 freight operations at scale.
NL equivalent: Action 1.2 (RDW fast-track template) directly mirrors TSFS 2021:4. Achievable in 0–6 months without new legislation.
The key difference between NL and both precedents: Germany and Sweden each made one deliberate political decision — to define who is legally responsible when no human driver is present. The Netherlands has not made that decision. Every other barrier flows from that single gap. This project proposes making that decision now.

16 — Conclusion

The answer — and what needs to happen next

Research Question — Answered

"Can Physical Internet hubs be implemented in Dutch ports within a 12-month deployment timeline given current autonomous vehicle regulations?"

No.

Not under current regulations. The Netherlands scores 0/10 on the Regulatory Readiness Index across all five dimensions — the only country in this analysis where no regulatory prerequisite for Physical Internet hub deployment is in place. Germany scores 8/10 and Sweden 6/10 using the same technology. The gap is not technical. It is a policy choice.

What the research found

Three interconnected regulatory barriers block deployment: the RDW case-by-case approval process (R1, 55% of sources), the unresolved Vienna Convention Article 8 gap (R2), and the absent liability framework that prevents insurance market development (R3/R4). These are not independent problems — resolving R1 through a sandbox directly begins breaking the R3/R4 circular barrier.

What the evidence proves

Technology is not the constraint. Einride L4 trucks are commercially deployed and RISE-audited. TNO confirms readiness in controlled port zones. Germany and Sweden demonstrate that the EU legal framework permits deployment — the difference is entirely national regulatory approach. Van Duin et al. (2023) confirms stakeholders support reform if nationally coordinated, not individually imposed.

What needs to happen

Two phases. Phase 1 (0–12 months): RDW fast-track template + Maasvlakte sandbox under Article 39 — no new law required. Phase 2 (12–24 months): dedicated L4 AV law + WVW amendment with Technical Operator role. If approved, NL reaches 9/10 readiness within 24 months and is positioned to lead the EU hub-to-hub deployment framework the European Commission targets for 2026.

✓
Final Statement

The Netherlands has world-class port infrastructure, operational AV technology, and active TNO research programmes. The only thing missing is the regulatory framework to connect them. This project proposes that framework. A Maasvlakte sandbox in 2026 and a national L4 law by 2027 would bring the Netherlands from last place in European AV freight regulation to alignment with its main competitors — and position Rotterdam as the first EU port to operate a fully autonomous Physical Internet hub at commercial scale.
