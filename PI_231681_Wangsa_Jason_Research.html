01 — Key Research Findings

Five findings. One clear answer.

Document analysis, comparative case, and readiness index all converge: the barrier is regulatory, not technical or economic.

F1
Primary Barrier · R1 · 55% of sources

The RDW case-by-case approval process is the operational bottleneck

Coded R1 in 11 of 20 sources. Tesla FSD approval: 18+ months still pending March 2026. Single-operator exemptions take 6–12 months (TNO, 2025). A PI hub requiring multi-operator coordination would multiply this timeline significantly under current rules. No standardised multi-operator pathway exists.

RDW (2025) · TNO (2025) · Electrek (2026) · Galassi et al. (2023)
Primary barrier
11
of 20 sources
F2
Vienna Convention · R2 · 35% of sources

Article 8 gap unresolved in Dutch national law

Vellinga (2019): an AV has no 'driver' under Art. 1(v) of the Vienna Convention — making L4 operation non-compliant with Dutch road traffic law as written. Germany resolved this via the Technical Supervisor role in the 2021 Act. The Netherlands has not acted. RDW explicitly prohibits remote operation without a safety driver on site.

Vellinga (2019) · WEF (2019) · German AV Act (2021) · RDW (2025)
Primary barrier

F3
Circular Barrier · R3/R4 · 8 sources combined

Liability-insurance loop reinforces the deployment blockage

No L4 liability rule in Dutch law → insurers cannot price policies → operators cannot deploy without coverage → no actuarial data → loop continues. Resolving the RDW approval pathway (F1) would break this loop by generating deployment data. Secondary to R1 but reinforcing the overall barrier structure.

Tjong Tjin Tai (2024) · Munich Re (n.d.) · Tjong Tjin Tai (2018)
Secondary barrier

F4
Technology Confirmed Ready · T · 6 sources

Technology is NOT the constraint — it is commercially deployed

Einride's L4 freight trucks commercially deployed on fixed EU routes, independently audited by RISE (2024). Milakis et al. (2017): NL deployment divergence driven entirely by regulatory approach, not technical capability. TNO confirms technology ready in controlled port zones (APM Maasvlakte trials active).

Einride/RISE (2024) · Milakis et al. (2017) · TNO (2025) · WEF (2025)

F5
Counter-Evidence — Refines Hypothesis

Stakeholders support regulation — if nationally coordinated

Van Duin et al. (2023) found stakeholders positive toward regulation if nationally coordinated. This refines — not disproves — the hypothesis. The problem is regulatory fragmentation, not regulation itself. Germany and Sweden both regulate predictably. NL regulates case-by-case and unpredictably. Source coded as-written, not excluded.

02 — Comparative Case Analysis

Netherlands vs Germany vs Sweden

All three countries share comparable AV technology and EU regulatory context. Deployment differences are explained entirely by national regulatory choices.

Because technology is held constant across all three cases, any difference in deployment outcomes must be attributed to national regulatory differences. This design directly tests whether Dutch regulatory barriers are the key variable blocking deployment — and the evidence confirms they are.

About This Research

Research design & methodology

This research used three complementary methods, each building on the last, to answer the research question from multiple angles.

M1
Document Analysis
20 sources, 8 barrier codes
Academic papers, policy documents, and industry reports coded against R1–R4, T, E, C, and S barrier categories. Purposive sampling. Counter-evidence included deliberately.
M2
Comparative Case Analysis
Netherlands vs Germany vs Sweden
Countries selected for comparable AV technology and EU membership. Technology held constant isolates national regulatory differences as the explanatory variable for deployment outcomes.
M3
Regulatory Readiness Index
0–2 scoring across 5 dimensions
Converts qualitative findings from M1 and M2 into a structured, measurable output. Score is traceable back to specific source citations — not a subjective ranking.
